# Week 7: Mapping Inputs to Output Performance
*Unit 2 — The Chemical Plant*

This week is the unit's mini-capstone. You'll take everything you've learned about fuel and observe your own system over several days.

**Data is more honest than memory. Most of us think we know how we feel during the day — but when we actually write it down, the patterns are often different than we expected. The goal of this week is to collect honest data about your own inputs and outputs, and look for patterns. No judgment, no grading. Just observation.**

This is also a practice run for the capstone in Weeks 15–18.

---

:::warning Safety and Privacy Reminder
This lesson helps you notice patterns. It does not diagnose, treat, or prescribe anything. Your body data is private, and you do not have to share it with a group. If something feels scary, painful, extreme, or confusing, pause and talk to a trusted adult or healthcare professional.
:::

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This week is data collection — three to four days of tracking.
- Critical reminder: this is NOT a food-tracking exercise for diet purposes. It is a systems audit. Inputs and outputs.
- The learner records what they noticed coming in and what they noticed coming out. They look for correlations.
- Do not, under any circumstances, use this week to push food behavior change. The point is to see the patterns. Family decisions about food belong to family.
- Some learners may notice patterns about specific foods. That's interesting data, not a verdict.
- Any learner may choose the non-food version of this audit for any reason, and they do not have to explain why.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Plan the audit around a typical week. Don't pick a week with travel, holidays, or unusual events.
- Have a simple tracking sheet ready (the format is in Session 1, and the [Printable Templates](./printable-templates.md) page has both food-inclusive and food-free versions).
- **Important framing reminder:** This is not a food diary. It's a system audit. We're tracking inputs (food, sleep, activity) AND outputs (energy, mood, focus). Both sides of the loop.
- Any learner may choose the food-free version of the audit for any reason. They do not have to explain why. Food-free options include sleep, energy, focus, hydration cues, and environmental patterns.
- No calorie counting, no public sharing of logs, and no behavior-change assignments based on the audit.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
The most important phrase this week is: **"Just notice."**

We're not trying to fix anything. We're not deciding anything. We're collecting data. The patterns are interesting in themselves. Whether to do something about a pattern is a completely separate question that doesn't belong in this curriculum.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "We're going to keep a log for a few days of what you ate and how you felt, and then look at it together to see if we notice anything."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Use 2 days of tracking instead of 3 or 4.
- Use simple categories instead of specific foods: "carb meal," "balanced meal," "snack."
- Skip the correlation discussion. The activity of just SEEING the data is enough at this age.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Have the learner draw a smiley/neutral/frowny face for each meal's energy rating.
- The facilitator can do most of the writing.

**What success looks like:** The learner notices one thing about their own pattern (e.g., "I always get sleepy after lunch" or "I feel best in the morning after eggs"). That's a real observation.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Track for 4 full days minimum.
- Plot the data on real graphs.
- Calculate simple correlations — e.g., "On days I had high-carb breakfast, my afternoon energy was X. On days I had balanced breakfast, it was Y."
- Discuss the difference between correlation and causation. Just because two things appeared together doesn't prove one caused the other. (Important systems thinking skill.)
:::

## Guided Session 1
### Designing the Audit
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- explain the difference between inputs and outputs in a system audit
- design a simple data tracking sheet
- commit to honest data collection

---

#### Activities

**1. The Audit Concept**

Ask:

> "What does the word 'audit' mean?"

It's a careful examination of records to see what's actually happening. Companies audit their finances. Engineers audit systems. We're going to audit YOUR system for a few days.

> "We're going to collect data on what goes IN (food, sleep, water, activity) and what comes OUT (energy, focus, mood, physical performance). Then we'll look at the data and see if there are any patterns."

Two equally valid versions of this audit:

- **Food-inclusive audit:** meals are one type of input among several.
- **Food-free audit:** skip meals completely and track sleep, focus, hydration cues, energy, activity, and environment only.

---

**2. The Tracking Sheet**

Together, design a tracking sheet. Here's a starting template:

```
DATE: ____________

INPUTS:
- Sleep last night: ___ hours
- Breakfast: ____________________ (what + when)
- Lunch: _______________________ (what + when)
- Dinner: ______________________ (what + when)
- Snacks: ______________________ (what + when)
- Water intake (just rough): low / medium / high
- Physical activity: ___________ (what + how long)

OUTPUTS (rate 1–10 four times during the day):
- Morning energy: ___
- Late morning energy: ___
- Mid-afternoon energy: ___
- Evening energy: ___

- Morning focus: ___
- Afternoon focus: ___

- Mood overall: ___

ANYTHING ELSE I NOTICED TODAY:
_______________________________________________
_______________________________________________
```

If using the food-free version, replace the meal lines with things like bedtime consistency, morning light, hydration cues, environment notes, or activity patterns.

You can simplify or expand this depending on the learner. Keep it short enough that they'll actually do it.

---

**3. The "Just Notice" Commitment**

Important conversation before starting:

> "When you're tracking, you might be tempted to change what you eat or what you do because you know it's being recorded. Try NOT to do that. Just live your normal week, and write down what actually happens. The data is only useful if it's honest."

> "Also: nobody is going to grade this. Not me, not anyone. You're the only person looking at it. So you can be totally honest."

> "You also do not have to share the sheet with anyone else. The data is not a grade, and your body is not being judged."

Discussion question: "If you weren't being recorded, what would a typical Tuesday look like? Just go live that."

## Guided Session 2
### Reading the Patterns
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- look at their own data and identify at least one pattern
- distinguish correlation from causation
- form a hypothesis (a guess) about what's driving a pattern

---

#### Activities

**1. Spread Out the Data**

After 3–4 days of tracking, lay all the daily sheets out on a table side by side.

Ask the learner:

> "Look across all the days. Do you see anything that's similar from day to day?"

Examples of what they might notice:

- "My energy always drops in the afternoon."
- "I had the best focus on the day I slept the longest."
- "I felt the worst on the day I had the latest dinner."
- "My mood was best on the day I was outside the most."

Just spot the patterns. Don't analyze yet.

---

**2. Form Hypotheses**

Now turn each pattern into a **hypothesis** — a testable guess about what's causing it.

| Pattern | Hypothesis (a guess about WHY) |
|---|---|
| My afternoon energy was lowest on the days I had pasta for lunch | Maybe the simple-carb lunch causes a glucose crash later |
| My focus was best on the day I slept 10 hours | Maybe my brain needs more sleep than I usually give it |
| I felt grumpy on the day I drank almost no water | Maybe dehydration was making the brain sluggish |

> "Notice that hypotheses are GUESSES. We don't know yet. We'd need more data to be sure. But the guess is the starting point for understanding what's happening."

---

**3. Correlation vs. Causation (For Older Learners)**

> "Two things happening together doesn't mean one caused the other. If you ate pizza for lunch AND played soccer outside AND had a great afternoon, was it the pizza? The soccer? The fresh air? The fact that you had a good day already? All of those? You can't tell from one day."

This is an important systems thinking skill: don't jump to conclusions from a single data point.

To actually test a hypothesis, you'd need to:

- Change ONE thing and keep everything else the same.
- Observe what happens.
- Repeat several times to be sure it wasn't random.

That's what scientists do. That's what you'll do in the capstone (Weeks 15–18).

For now, just notice the patterns and form your guesses.

## Independent Practice

### Goal

Complete 3–4 days of audit data. Then sit with the data and write up what you notice.

### Activities

**1. The Audit**

Use the tracking sheet from Session 1. Fill it out at the end of each day, OR throughout the day if that works better. Do this for 3–4 days minimum.

If a day goes sideways and you don't fill in the sheet, just skip that day. Don't try to reconstruct it from memory. Honest data only.

If the tracking starts to increase anxiety or feel too personal, switch to the food-free version or stop. That is allowed.

**2. The Pattern Hunt**

After 3–4 days, lay out the data and answer these questions in your Bio-Telemetry Log:

- What's ONE pattern I noticed across the days?
- What's my best hypothesis for why that pattern exists?
- What would I need to do to TEST that hypothesis?

### Bio-Telemetry Log

> **The Pattern I Noticed:** _____________
>
> **My Hypothesis for Why:** _____________
>
> **What I'd Need to Test It:** _____________
>
> **One Thing I'm Curious About Now:** _____________

**Sentence starters for younger learners:**
- "I noticed that on days when ___, I felt ___."
- "I think maybe ___ is what's happening."

### Reflection Questions

- Did anything in your data surprise you?
- Were you tempted to change things because you knew they were being tracked?
- If you ran this audit for a whole month, what do you think you'd find?

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Explain what an audit is:** "Watching the system and writing down what happens, so you can see patterns." Or similar.
2. **Distinguish input from output:** "Inputs are what go in — food, sleep, water. Outputs are what comes out — energy, mood, focus." Or similar.
3. **Form a hypothesis from their own data:** Any reasonable guess that connects a pattern to a possible cause.

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 8.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
> "How did it feel to track your inputs and outputs for a few days? Did anything change about how you noticed your own body?"

> "There's a real risk with this kind of tracking — that you start judging your inputs ('I shouldn't have eaten that') or feel bad when the data is inconsistent. If you noticed any of that, set it aside. The data is information. It's not a grade."

This unit ends with the data audit because the next step in a real engineer's process is: collect data, observe, then decide what to do. This curriculum stops at the OBSERVE step. What to do with the data is up to you and your family.

**This week's takeaway:** Your system is observable. Your patterns are real. You can study yourself the way a scientist studies any system — with curiosity, not judgment.
:::
