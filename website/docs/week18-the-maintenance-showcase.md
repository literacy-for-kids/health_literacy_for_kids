# Week 18: Presenting Your Optimization Protocol
*Unit 5 — The Optimization Protocol (Capstone) — Finale*

The experiment is over. Now you make sense of it, and you share what you choose to share.

**For two weeks, you ran a real experiment on your own body. You picked the variable, designed the protocol, set up the environment, and collected the data. This week, you analyze the data, write Protocol v2.0 with everything you'd change next time, and present your findings to a real audience if you want. This is what scientists do — not as a metaphor, but as the actual scientific method. You ran a study. Now you decide how to share the result.**

This is the last week. Take your time with it.

---

:::warning Safety and Privacy Reminder
This lesson helps you notice patterns. It does not diagnose, treat, or prescribe anything. Your body data is private, and you do not have to share it with a group. A showcase can use anonymized, fictionalized, partial, or fully private data.
:::

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This is the finale of 18 weeks of work. Celebrate it. Loudly.
- The student ran a real experiment on themselves. They followed a protocol, collected data, analyzed it, and drew conclusions. That is the scientific method — not as abstract concept, but as something they actually did. Name this for them.
- The presentation does not need to be polished. A 3-minute conversation with a family member is enough. A poster board is great. A slide deck is great. Acting it out is great. Format is up to the student.
- The final Bio-Telemetry Log entry is the equivalent of a "letter to your past self." Give the student real time and space for it.
- This is also the closure for the entire curriculum, not just the capstone. The closing recap should look across all 18 weeks, not just the past four.
- Celebrate the process, not the outcome. Protocol v2.0 is not a self-improvement contract.
- The learner chooses what to share and what to keep private.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Have the student's complete dataset and Bio-Telemetry Log ready before Session 1 starts. If anything is missing, gather it before the session.
- Re-read the Week 15 protocol and Week 16 environmental changes. The "before/after" comparison this week depends on knowing what was set up at the start.
- Decide who the showcase audience will be. Family member? Sibling? Just you and the student? Multiple people if available. Even one real audience member is enough.
- Prepare a small but real celebration. This is the end of a long curriculum. The student carried out the whole thing.
- Ask the learner what they want to share, anonymize, fictionalize, or keep private before any presentation begins.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
You are about to celebrate a real accomplishment. The student followed a protocol, collected data, analyzed it, and drew conclusions about themselves. That is science. Treat it that way.

**Most important thing to remember:** It does not matter whether the protocol "worked." What matters is that the student ran a real experiment, with real data, on something they were curious about. A null result — "no change" — is also a real scientific finding.

Protocol v2.0 is a learning document, not a promise to self-optimize.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "You ran an experiment for two weeks. Now you get to tell people what you found out!"

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Skip the formal Protocol v2.0 structure. Use a simple "What worked / What didn't / What I'd try next time" three-column layout.
- The showcase can be a show-and-tell, 3 minutes, with drawings.
- The final journal entry can be one sentence and a drawing.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Use the tracking sheet as the visual aid for the presentation — just hold it up and walk through it.
- Practice the presentation once with a stuffed animal audience before doing it for real.

**What success looks like:** The learner can answer four questions in their own words: "What was I studying? What did I do? What happened? What would I do next time?"
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Full Protocol v2.0 document with sections for What's New, Bug Fixes, Known Issues, and Removed.
- Formal showcase with the full 5-question structure (variable, baseline, protocol, data, v2.0).
- Encourage the student to identify which earlier curriculum concepts showed up in their experiment ("This was a feedback loop / This used the maintenance cycle idea / This connected to circadian rhythms").
:::

## Guided Session 1
### The Data Analysis
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- compile and summarize their two-week dataset
- compare experimental data against their Week 15 baseline
- write Protocol v2.0 — what they'd change, what they'd keep, what they'd study next

---

#### Activities

**1. Spread Out the Evidence**

Lay everything on the table:

- The Week 15 protocol (the original plan)
- The Week 15 baseline measurements (the "before" picture)
- The Week 16 environmental change notes
- The full 14-day tracking sheet (the "after" data)
- The Bio-Telemetry Log

This is your evidence. Engineers and scientists do this — they lay everything out in physical space so they can see the full picture at once.

---

**2. The Before / After Comparison**

Build a simple comparison together. Don't overcomplicate the math.

```
BEFORE (Week 15 Baseline, 3 days)
  Average metric reading: ___
  Range (lowest to highest): ___ to ___
  Adherence: N/A (no protocol yet)

DURING EXPERIMENT (Weeks 16–17, ~14 days)
  Average metric reading: ___
  Range (lowest to highest): ___ to ___
  Adherence: ___% of days marked Y or P
  Number of days tracked: ___ / 14
```

Now look at it together:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Did the average move? In which direction? | |
| Was the change small, medium, or large? | |
| Was there more variation, less variation, or about the same? | |
| On days you followed the protocol (Y), did the metric look different than on days you didn't (N)? | |
| Did any context notes correlate with unusual readings? | |
| What's the most surprising thing in the data? | |

---

**3. Honest Interpretation**

Before writing Protocol v2.0, the student answers three questions out loud:

> "What does the data clearly show?"

> "What does the data NOT show? (What can't I conclude from two weeks?)"

> "Is there a result that I was hoping for that the data doesn't actually support?"

That last one is hard. It's the part most adults skip — the willingness to notice that the data didn't say what you wanted it to. Honest scientists do this on purpose.

If the data shows no change, that's a valid finding. Some possible reasons (let the student think through them):

- Two weeks may not be long enough for this variable.
- The protocol may not have been the right intervention.
- The environment may have changed in some other way that masked the effect.
- The metric may not capture what the student actually cared about.
- Some variables are genuinely stable and don't respond to small changes — that's a real fact about that variable.

Any of these is a legitimate scientific conclusion.

---

**4. Write Protocol v2.0**

Now the student writes the patch — just like a software engineer writing patch notes after testing the latest build.

```
PROTOCOL v2.0 — PATCH NOTES

VARIABLE I WAS STUDYING:
_______________________________________

WHAT I KEPT (from v1.0):
- [Parts that worked and should stay]
- [Parts that worked and should stay]

WHAT I CHANGED:
- [Specific change, based on data or experience]
- [Specific change, based on data or experience]

WHAT I REMOVED:
- [Parts of v1.0 that turned out to not matter or to be too hard]

NEW QUESTIONS THIS EXPERIMENT RAISED:
- [Things I'm now curious about that I wasn't curious about before]
- [Things I'd study next, if I kept going]

WHAT I'D STUDY NEXT (if I ran a v3.0):
_______________________________________
```

This is real engineering documentation. The protocol is now a living thing — version 1.0 tested, version 2.0 ready, version 3.0 imaginable.

## Guided Session 2
### The Showcase
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- present their experiment clearly to a real audience
- answer audience questions about their process and findings
- describe what they did using the language of the scientific method

---

#### Activities

**1. Prepare the Presentation**

A clean 3–5 minute presentation, in the student's chosen format. Structure:

1. **What variable did I study?** ("I was studying my morning energy / my sleep / my focus during homework / etc.")
2. **What was my baseline?** ("Before the experiment, my average reading was about ___.")
3. **What protocol did I follow?** ("My one specific change was ___. I also adjusted my environment by ___.")
4. **What did the data show?** ("Over the two weeks, my average was ___. Adherence was ___. The biggest pattern I noticed was ___.")
5. **What would I do in Version 2.0?** ("If I ran this again, I would keep ___ and change ___.")

Format options:

- Paper or poster board with the tracking sheet and a before/after chart
- A few simple slides
- Just a verbal walk-through, holding up the tracking sheet
- A drawn diagram of the experiment
- An anonymized or fictionalized version that protects private details
- A partial-data version that only shows selected parts of the dataset
- A process-only version focused on what the learner discovered about experiments, without personal body details

Style is up to the student. The structure is what matters.

---

**2. Practice Once**

Before the real audience, the student does a single practice run — to the facilitator, to a stuffed animal, to a mirror. Anything. The goal is to find the parts where they get stuck so they can smooth those parts before presenting for real.

---

**3. Present!**

The real presentation. To the chosen audience: a family member, a sibling, a friend, the facilitator, or a small group.

After the presentation, the audience asks 2 or 3 questions. Useful audience prompts:

- "What was the hardest part of running this experiment?"
- "Did anything in the data surprise you?"
- "What would you do differently if you started over?"
- "Are you going to keep doing any of the things from your protocol after the experiment is over?"
- "If you had a year to study yourself instead of two weeks, what would you want to study?"

The student answers. Wrong answers don't exist here. Their data is their data.

---

**4. The Celebration**

This is a real accomplishment. Celebrate it on purpose.

> "Over 18 weeks, you learned how the body keeps itself in balance, how fuel becomes energy, how the immune system identifies threats, how sleep rebuilds the brain, how the microbiome shapes mood, and how exercise works at a cellular level. Then you took everything you learned and you ran an experiment on yourself. You designed a protocol, set up an environment, collected data for two weeks, analyzed it, and drew conclusions. That is the scientific method. You didn't just learn about it — you did it."

If the family wants to mark this with a small certificate, a special meal, a sticker, a photo of the student holding the Bio-Telemetry Log, or any other small ritual — this is the moment. The closing of a curriculum should feel like a real ending.

## Independent Practice

### Goal

Write the final entry in the Bio-Telemetry Log. A letter to the version of yourself that started this curriculum.

### Activities

**1. The Final Bio-Telemetry Log Entry**

Open the Bio-Telemetry Log to a fresh page. This entry is the closing of the entire 18-week log.

Answer these questions in your own words. Take your time. No length requirement — but it should feel real.

1. **What did I understand about my body at Week 1?** Try to remember honestly. Most people at Week 1 think of the body as one thing that just "works."

2. **What do I understand about my body now?** Be specific. Name systems. Name mechanisms.

3. **What was the single most surprising thing I learned in this whole curriculum?** Could be a fact. Could be an insight. Could be something I noticed about myself.

4. **What system in my body do I now think about differently?** (immune system, sleep, fuel and glucose, microbiome, stress response, circadian rhythm, etc.)

5. **What's something I want to keep doing after this curriculum is over?** This is voluntary. It might be tracking. It might be one of the environmental changes. It might be nothing — and that's also fine.

6. **Letter to my Week 1 self.** Write a short message to the version of yourself that started this curriculum. What do you wish you'd known then?

---

**2. The "Then vs. Now" Sentence**

Write one sentence in each blank:

> "When I started this curriculum, I thought my body was ____________."
>
> "Now I think my body is ____________."

---

**3. The Last Page**

On the very last page of the Bio-Telemetry Log, write the date and sign your name. Like a real scientist closing a research notebook.

### Bio-Telemetry Log

> **THIS IS the journal entry for this week. Take your time.**

### Reflection Questions

- If you taught this curriculum to a friend, which week would you start with? Why?
- Are you going to keep the Bio-Telemetry Log? Why or why not?
- In 5 years, which idea from this curriculum do you think will still matter to you?

---

## Check for Understanding

This is the final check for the entire curriculum. After the presentation and the final Log entry, confirm:

1. **Concept retention:** "Name 3 big ideas from this curriculum." (Looking for any 3 — homeostasis, feedback loops, immune response, sleep architecture, microbiome, glucose regulation, stress response, environmental cues, the scientific method as a personal tool, etc.)
2. **Personal framework:** "If you noticed something weird about how you were feeling — say, you were unusually tired for a week — what's one or two things you might think about?" (Looking for: any systems-level thinking. "I'd think about my sleep. I'd think about whether I was getting sick. I'd think about what changed in my environment recently.")
3. **Transfer to life:** "Tell me about something you noticed about your own body during this curriculum that you wouldn't have noticed before." (Looking for: ANY real example, no matter how small.)
4. **Growth mindset:** "What's something you used to think about your body that you've changed your mind about?" (Looking for: evidence of real reframing — "I didn't know there was anything happening when I slept" or "I thought the immune system was just one thing.")

If the learner can show concept retention, a personal framework, one real-world observation, and self-awareness about growth — they have completed the curriculum successfully. Celebrate.

## Looking Across All 18 Weeks

:::tip What Success Looks Like Across the Curriculum
By the end of the curriculum, the student should be able to recognize the core idea of each unit. This is not a quiz — it's a way to look back and see how much ground was covered.

| Unit | Weeks | What the student should now understand |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit 1 — The Hardware** | 1–3 | The body is a self-regulating system. Homeostasis is the goal. Feedback loops are the mechanism. Baseline telemetry is how we observe the system. |
| **Unit 2 — Fuel Processing** | 4–7 | Food is fuel. Macronutrients are processed differently. Glucose and insulin form a feedback loop. The fuel system can be audited. |
| **Unit 3 — Threat Response** | 8–10 | The immune system identifies and responds to threats. Inflammation and fever are intentional responses, not malfunctions. An illness can be modeled as a system event with a beginning, middle, and end. |
| **Unit 4 — Preventative Maintenance** | 11–14 | Circadian rhythms align internal processes with the external day. Sleep is when most maintenance happens. The microbiome is a third-party processing system the body depends on. Exercise works through a wear-and-repair cycle. |
| **Unit 5 — The Optimization Protocol (Capstone)** | 15–18 | Scientific method as a personal tool. Design a protocol. Tune the environment. Run the experiment. Analyze the data. Iterate. |
:::

:::tip Vocabulary You Can Now Use
- **Homeostasis** — the body's set point
- **Feedback loop** — sensor → controller → actuator
- **Macronutrient** — carbs, fats, proteins
- **Insulin** — glucose regulation hormone
- **Immune response** — the body's threat-detection system
- **Circadian rhythm** — the body's internal 24-hour clock
- **Sleep architecture** — the structured stages of sleep
- **Microbiome** — the ecosystem of microbes that live in you
- **Allostasis** — the body's ability to adapt to changing demands
- **Bio-telemetry** — measuring the body's signals
:::

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
> "Eighteen weeks ago, you started this curriculum. You probably had a vague sense that your body 'worked' but not much sense of HOW. Now you can describe what a fever is doing. You can explain why sleep matters at a cellular level. You can describe what your gut microbes are doing for you right now. You can describe what happens to your body during a stressful moment, and what happens during the recovery from one. That is a remarkable amount of learning."

> "But here is the bigger thing. You're not just someone who knows facts about the body. You're someone who knows how to STUDY one — your own. You ran an experiment. You collected data. You drew conclusions. That habit — being curious about your own body, paying attention to the signals it sends, asking questions instead of guessing — that habit is the actual point of this whole curriculum."

> "Most adults never learn this. They live in bodies they don't understand and don't observe. You're 18 weeks ahead of that. Keep going."

**Curriculum-wide takeaway:** Your body is a system. You can understand it. You can observe it. You can run experiments on it. You can keep doing this for the rest of your life — not as a project, but as a habit. That's bio-telemetry literacy.
:::
