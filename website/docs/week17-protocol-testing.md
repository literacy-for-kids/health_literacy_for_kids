# Week 17: Running the Experiment and Collecting Data
*Unit 5 — The Optimization Protocol (Capstone)*

This is the part where you do the science.

**You wrote a protocol in Week 15. You set up the environment in Week 16. For the next two weeks, you run the experiment. Every day, you collect a small amount of data on your target variable. You stick to the protocol as well as you can. Halfway through, you check in to see what's working and what isn't, and you adjust if you need to. At the end of two weeks, you'll have a real dataset about yourself, generated by you, in the real world. That's a science project.**

This is the longest "single" week in the curriculum — it runs across two full weeks of tracking. Pace yourself.

---

:::warning Safety and Privacy Reminder
This lesson helps you notice patterns. It does not diagnose, treat, or prescribe anything. Your body data is private, and you do not have to share it with a group. If tracking increases anxiety, simplify the protocol, switch to observation-only, or pause.
:::

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- Two weeks of daily data collection. Daily tracking should be small — 2 to 3 minutes maximum.
- Two facilitator touchpoints: one at the start ("Setting Up the Tracking System") and one around the midpoint ("Mid-Course Review").
- The midpoint check is not optional. Many students will hit a friction point in the first week. If the protocol is unworkable, fix it now — don't wait until Week 18 to discover it was broken.
- Honest framing matters: two weeks is short for many health variables. The goal isn't "did I get dramatically better." It's "can I see a pattern in my own data."
- Tracking consistency is the actual achievement of this week. A student who tracked every day and saw no change has run a successful experiment.
- Missing data is not failure, and the dataset stays private unless the learner chooses to share it.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Re-read the student's Week 15 protocol and Week 16 environmental changes. You'll need both fresh in your mind.
- Decide when the midpoint check-in will happen and put it on the calendar. Roughly 7 days after tracking starts.
- Be ready for the protocol to need adjustment. That's normal. A protocol that can't be followed is a protocol with a design problem, not a moral failing.
- Keep tracking small. If a student is trying to record more than three things per day, that's a sign the system is too complex.
- The learner does not have to share the tracking sheet with anyone else. Treat it like private science data.
- If tracking increases anxiety, simplify the protocol or stop. That is useful information about the protocol.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
You are now a research advisor, not a coach. The experiment is running. Your job is to help the student see what the data is showing — without pushing toward a particular conclusion.

**Best opening question for the midpoint check:** "What's the data showing so far? And what's the experience of doing this been like?"
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "Every day for the next two weeks, you'll write one small thing down about how you're doing."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- One metric, not three. Maximum.
- Use a happy face / medium face / sad face scale, or a 1–3 scale instead of 1–10.
- Tracking can be done at the same time every day (e.g., right after dinner) so it becomes a routine.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Make the tracking sheet visual — a calendar grid where the learner draws or stamps a face for each day.
- The midpoint review can be a 5-minute conversation, not a session.

**What success looks like:** The learner records something most days for two weeks. Even partial tracking shows them a pattern.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Use a full tracking sheet with date, metric reading, adherence (yes / partial / no), and context notes.
- At the midpoint, the student should be able to read their own data and describe a pattern in words ("most days I'm around 6, but Mondays are lower").
- Encourage tracking adherence as its own metric — "how often did I actually follow the protocol?" — alongside the target variable.
:::

## Guided Session 1
### Setting Up the Tracking System
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- design a simple daily tracking sheet for their metric
- specify when and how they will record each day's data
- distinguish between metric, adherence, and context — and know why all three matter

---

#### Activities

**1. The Three Things to Track**

A useful tracking sheet has three columns plus a date. Walk through them with the student.

| Column | What it captures | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Date** | What day this entry is for | So you can put the days in order later |
| **Metric** | The actual measurement of your variable (e.g., 1–10 morning energy, time to fall asleep, etc.) | The thing you're studying |
| **Adherence** | Did you follow the protocol today? yes / partial / no | Lets you see whether the protocol is even being tested |
| **Context** | Any unusual event that might have affected the metric today (sick, big test, late event, traveled, fight with a friend, holiday food, etc.) | Without this, weird data points look mysterious |

> "Imagine you only tracked the metric. You'd see a low number on a Tuesday and have no idea why. Was the protocol skipped? Were you sick? Was it just a bad day? Adherence and context turn mystery data into useful data."

---

**2. The Tracking Sheet**

Design the sheet. Keep it simple. Here's a template — the student can adapt it to their variable.

```
TRACKING SHEET — Variable: ________________________

| Date  | Metric | Adherence (Y/P/N) | Context notes              |
|-------|--------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
| ___   |        |                   |                            |
```

Fourteen rows for fourteen days. The student can draw this in the Bio-Telemetry Log, put it on a single page in a notebook, or use a simple digital version (a note app, a spreadsheet) — whatever they'll actually fill in.

---

**3. The Daily Ritual**

> "When and where are you going to do the tracking?"

Tracking has to attach to an existing routine, or it won't happen. Some options:

- **Morning tracking:** Right after waking, for metrics like sleep quality, morning energy, or how rested you feel.
- **Evening tracking:** Right after dinner or right before bed, for daytime metrics like focus, mood, energy patterns, or hunger.
- **Twice-daily:** Some metrics need two measurements (e.g., morning energy AND afternoon energy). If so, attach each one to a clear cue.

The whole tracking event should take 2 to 3 minutes. If it takes longer than that, the system is too complex and the student will abandon it.

The student writes down their tracking plan:

> "I will record my data at ___ (time/cue) each day. The sheet lives at ___ (physical location or app)."

---

**4. The First Entry**

Do today's entry together, right now. Walk through how to read the metric, how to assess adherence, what counts as context.

This serves two purposes: it tests whether the system works in practice, and it marks the official start of the data collection period.

## Guided Session 2
### Mid-Course Review
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- look at their own data and describe a pattern they see
- assess their own adherence honestly
- identify a friction point in the protocol and adjust if needed

---

> **When to do this session:** About 7 days after the tracking starts. Roughly the midpoint of the two-week run.

---

#### Activities

**1. Read the Data Out Loud**

Have the student pull out the tracking sheet and read every entry out loud — date, metric, adherence, context. Don't analyze yet. Just read.

This is harder than it sounds. Most people don't actually look at the data they collect. Reading it aloud forces the student to notice gaps, surprising patterns, and what's actually there.

---

**2. The First Three Questions**

After reading, ask:

| Question | What you're looking for |
|---|---|
| "What pattern do you see, if any?" | A simple description: "Most days I'm around 6, but weekends are higher." Or "I really can't tell yet." Both are fine. |
| "Has your adherence been mostly Y, P, or N?" | Honest self-assessment of whether the protocol is even being tested |
| "Anything in the context column you think is affecting the metric?" | Awareness of confounding factors |

If the student says "I don't see any pattern," that's a valid answer. Two weeks is short and a week is even shorter. Sometimes the answer at the midpoint is "still gathering."

---

**3. The Friction Point Diagnosis**

> "If your adherence is mostly Partial or No, what's getting in the way?"

A protocol that doesn't get followed is a protocol with a friction point — and a friction point is fixable. Common friction points:

| Friction | What it sounds like | Possible fix |
|---|---|---|
| Protocol is too ambitious | "It's too much to do every day" | Cut the protocol in half. The smaller version is the real protocol. |
| Wrong time of day | "I keep forgetting" | Attach the protocol to a clearer cue (right after a meal, right before brushing teeth, etc.) |
| Missing material | "I needed X and didn't have it" | Solve the materials problem. The protocol shouldn't depend on a thing the student doesn't reliably have. |
| Social blocker | "My family doesn't have the same schedule" | Adjust the protocol to fit the household, or have a conversation with the relevant family member. |
| Environment blocker | "My room makes this hard" | Revisit the Week 16 audit. Is there one more environmental tweak that would unlock this? |
| Too vague | "I wasn't sure what counted" | Tighten the metric or the protocol behavior. Specific beats vague. |

The student can adjust the protocol now. This is allowed and encouraged. If the protocol changes, write a quick note in the Bio-Telemetry Log:

> "Protocol adjustment, day ___: Changed ___ because ___."

---

**4. What's Surprising**

Two questions to close the midpoint session:

> "What's been easier than you expected?"

> "What's been harder than you expected?"

The first one matters. Often the student expects some part of the protocol to be hard and discovers it's fine. That's data too — it means the difficulty was anticipatory, not real.

The second one is where the friction lives. If something was harder than expected, that's where to focus the adjustment.

## Independent Practice

### Goal

Track every day. At the end of two weeks, you have a complete dataset.

### Activities

**1. Daily Tracking (the whole two weeks)**

Every day, do your 2–3 minute tracking ritual. Fill in the row. If you skip a day, leave it blank — don't backfill from memory. Honest data beats complete data.

If the tracking starts to feel too intense or too personal, simplify it or pause it. That is allowed.

If you adjust the protocol mid-experiment, note the date of the change in your Bio-Telemetry Log so the data still makes sense later.

**2. The End-of-Two-Weeks Compilation**

At the end of the second week, before Week 18 starts, compile the data:

- **Number of days tracked:** ___ out of 14
- **Average metric reading (just add them up and divide):** ___
- **Adherence rate:** ___% of days marked Y or P
- **Any obvious pattern you can already see:** ___

Don't try to do deep analysis yet — that's Week 18. Just have the totals ready.

**3. The Honesty Check**

In your Bio-Telemetry Log, write:

> "If I had to describe my experiment to a friend right now in three sentences, I would say: _____________"

Then write:

> "The thing I most want to know when I look at my data more carefully is: _____________"

### Bio-Telemetry Log

> **What I noticed in the first week:** _____________
>
> **What changed (or didn't) at the midpoint:** _____________
>
> **My adherence pattern (mostly Y / P / N, and why):** _____________
>
> **End of two weeks — number of days I tracked:** _____ / 14
>
> **End of two weeks — anything that surprised me:** _____________

**Sentence starters for younger learners:**
- "Most days I felt ___."
- "The hardest day was ___."
- "I noticed that ___ matters more than I thought."

### Reflection Questions

- Was tracking easier or harder than you expected? Why?
- Did you ever want to lie on the tracking sheet to look better? (Almost everyone does at some point. It's worth noticing.)
- What's your honest guess about what the full two weeks of data is going to show?

---

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Describe their dataset:** "I tracked for ___ out of 14 days. My average reading was about ___."
2. **Describe a pattern (or honestly say there isn't one yet):** "I noticed ___" or "I really can't tell yet from this much data."
3. **Identify at least one friction point and what they did about it:** "The hardest part was ___, so I changed ___ at the midpoint."

If the learner can do all 3 of these, they're ready for Week 18.

---

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
> "You just ran a real experiment on yourself for two weeks. Most adults have never done that. They have opinions about how their body works — they don't have data. You now have data."

> "Two weeks is a short time. Many of the variables you might be studying — sleep quality, energy, mood, focus — would need months of data, with much bigger sample sizes, to show clear effects in a real research study. So if your two weeks didn't show a dramatic change, that doesn't mean nothing happened or that you failed. It means you ran a small experiment, exactly the size of two weeks. That's the right size for a 12-year-old scientist."

> "If you tracked consistently, that's a real accomplishment regardless of what the numbers show. If you adjusted your protocol at the midpoint because it wasn't working, that's *also* a real accomplishment — it means you noticed and responded to evidence."

**This week's takeaway:** Real science is mostly the boring part — the daily data collection, the honesty about adherence, the midpoint adjustment. The dramatic conclusion comes from the boring part being done well.
:::

## Spiral Review

:::tip Connecting to Earlier Weeks
**From Week 2:** Feedback loops require a sensor (the metric), an interpretation step (the analysis), and an actuator (the adjustment). Your tracking system is exactly this structure — and the midpoint review is the feedback loop closing.

**From Week 3:** You took baseline telemetry data in Week 3. The data you're collecting now is comparable to that — it's the same kind of activity (measuring something about yourself, repeatedly, over time) at a more advanced level.

**From Week 7:** The fuel audit was a smaller version of this same skill. You looked at a system, tracked specific data points, and noticed patterns. Two weeks of tracking is the same skill at longer range.
:::

---

:::tip Simplify (Ages 8–9)
Drop adherence and context columns. Just track the metric. Use a happy face / medium face / sad face scale. The midpoint review can be a five-minute conversation: "How's it going? What's been the hardest?"
:::

:::tip Extend (Ages 10–12)
Add a second metric — a "control" variable that you wouldn't expect to change. For example, if you're studying sleep, also track something unrelated like hand strength or reading speed. If the control variable changes too, you've learned something interesting (something is shifting across your whole life). If it stays stable while your target variable moves, you've got a stronger result. This is how real studies build internal validity.
:::
