# Week 3: Measuring Your Own Hardware Recovery Rate
*Unit 1 — The Control Loops*

You've learned how the body's loops work. Now you're going to measure your own.

**A baseline is a starting measurement of a system in its normal state. Without a baseline, you cannot tell whether anything has changed. This week, you'll establish your personal baseline telemetry — the numbers that describe how your hardware operates at rest and how fast it recovers from a load.**

These numbers will become reference points for the rest of the curriculum. You'll come back to them again and again.

---

:::warning Note for Facilitators
This activity measures normal physiological parameters in a healthy child at rest and during light exercise. **It is an educational activity, not a medical assessment.** Treat the Bio-Telemetry Log like private science notes, not a scoreboard. If a student consistently shows unusual readings (very high resting heart rate, difficulty recovering, significant breathlessness from light activity), discuss this with a doctor. If the learner has a heart condition, asthma, or any medical reason to avoid moderate exertion, skip the exercise portion and focus on resting measurements only. If pain, dizziness, chest pain, breathing difficulty, or feeling unsafe shows up during the activity, stop immediately.
:::

---

:::warning Safety and Privacy Reminder
This lesson helps you notice patterns. It does not diagnose, treat, or prescribe anything. Your body data is private, and you do not have to share it with a group. If something feels scary, painful, extreme, or confusing, pause and talk to a trusted adult or healthcare professional.
:::

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This week is the first time the learner generates real data about themselves.
- Resting heart rate, breathing rate, and recovery time are the three measurements.
- Recovery time is the most interesting number. It tells you how fast the loops can bring the system back to baseline.
- These measurements will be repeated in Week 17. Save them carefully.
- Frame the numbers as DATA, not as a grade. There is no "good" or "bad" number — only their personal baseline.
- No comparison rule: the numbers are for understanding the learner's own system, not comparing them with anyone else.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Practice taking your own pulse so you can teach the technique. Two fingers (NOT your thumb — your thumb has its own pulse and will confuse the count) on the wrist below the base of the thumb, or on the side of the neck just below the jaw.
- Make sure the learner is rested and well-hydrated before the session. Caffeine, exercise in the last hour, or strong emotions will skew baseline readings.
- For the exercise component, have a clear, safe space. Move furniture if needed.
- Read the medical disclaimer at the top of this page and confirm the learner is comfortable participating.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
The learner may compare their numbers to a sibling, parent, or friend. Gently redirect: **these are your numbers**, not a competition. The point is to know your own system.

For reference, **healthy children ages 8–12 typically have a resting heart rate of about 70–100 beats per minute** and a **resting respiration rate of about 18–25 breaths per minute**. These are wide ranges. Both depend on the moment, the child, the hour. Numbers outside these ranges are not automatically a problem, but persistent unusual readings are worth discussing with a doctor.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "We're going to measure what your body does when it's resting, then when it works hard, then how fast it gets back to resting."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Use the 15-second count × 4 method rather than counting for a full minute (easier for younger learners).
- Skip the respiration tracking if it's confusing. Heart rate alone is plenty.
- Use 30 jumping jacks or just running in place for 30 seconds, whatever is easier.

**Adapting the activities:**
- The facilitator can count along with the learner.
- Use a "before" and "after" drawing of how the body feels, in addition to the numbers.
- Celebrate the fact that the heart rate goes back down on its own. That's the magic of the loop.

**What success looks like:** The learner can say that their heart beats faster when they exercise and goes back to normal when they rest, and they have at least one measured number recorded in the Log.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Use the full 60-second count for accuracy.
- Track all four post-exercise time points (immediate, 2 min, 5 min, 10 min).
- Calculate the recovery slope: how many bpm did the heart rate drop between each measurement?
- Discuss why a faster recovery rate generally indicates better cardiovascular conditioning (but emphasize: this is reference info, not a grade).
:::

## Guided Session 1
### Resting Telemetry
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- take their own pulse and count beats per minute
- count their breaths per minute
- record a resting baseline in the Bio-Telemetry Log

---

#### Activities

**1. The Pulse Technique**

Demonstrate first, on yourself:

- Place two fingers (index and middle) on the inside of your wrist, below the base of your thumb. Press gently.
- Feel for the steady "thump-thump-thump."
- Alternative spot: the side of the neck, just below the jaw and to one side of the windpipe.

Then guide the learner:

- "Can you feel yours?"
- It might take a minute to find. That's normal. Try the neck spot if the wrist is hard.
- Once they find it, have them just sit quietly and feel it for 30 seconds.

> "That's your heart pumping blood. About once every second, give or take. Right now, while you're sitting still."

---

**2. The Resting Heart Rate Measurement**

The protocol:

1. Sit quietly for 5 full minutes. No talking, no screens, no movement.
2. After 5 minutes, find the pulse.
3. Watch the clock or stopwatch. Count beats for 60 seconds.
4. Write the number down. This is the **resting heart rate**, measured in **beats per minute (bpm)**.

Alternative for younger learners: count for 15 seconds, multiply by 4.

Record in the Log:

> **Resting Heart Rate (Baseline):** _____ bpm
> **Date:** _____
> **Time of day:** _____

Discussion:

> "Your heart is pumping that many times every minute, every hour, every day. Without you thinking about it once. That's firmware."

For reference, share the typical range (70–100 bpm for children ages 8–12). Emphasize: this is a WIDE range. Your number is your number.

---

**3. The Resting Respiration Measurement**

Important: it's hard to measure your own breathing without changing it. The facilitator should count discreetly.

The protocol:

1. The learner sits quietly, looking at something neutral (not at the facilitator).
2. The facilitator watches the rise and fall of the chest or shoulders.
3. Count one full breath (in + out) as one breath.
4. Count for 60 seconds.

Record:

> **Resting Respiration Rate (Baseline):** _____ breaths per minute

Typical range for children 8–12: 18–25 breaths per minute.

Discussion:

> "Same idea. You're breathing about that many times every minute without thinking about it. That's another loop running."

## Guided Session 2
### The Recovery Test
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- safely complete a moderate exercise load
- measure how their heart rate changes immediately after exercise
- measure how long it takes for the heart rate to recover to baseline

---

#### Activities

**1. The Plan**

Walk the learner through what you're going to do, BEFORE you do it:

> "You're going to do 30 jumping jacks (or run in place for 60 seconds). It's a moderate amount of effort — not sprinting, not racing. After you stop, we're going to measure your heart rate four times:
> - Immediately when you stop
> - 2 minutes after you stop
> - 5 minutes after you stop
> - 10 minutes after you stop
>
> We're watching how fast your loops bring your heart rate back to baseline. That recovery is what we're studying."

Confirm the learner feels good and wants to do the activity. If they say no, switch to a lighter activity (a brisk walk for 60 seconds) or skip and use a different physiological event for baseline (e.g., a slow walk).

If pain, dizziness, chest pain, breathing difficulty, or feeling unsafe shows up at any point, stop immediately.

---

**2. The Exercise Load**

Have the learner do 30 jumping jacks (or jog in place for 60 seconds at moderate effort — they should be a little out of breath, not gasping). Then stop immediately and sit down.

---

**3. The Recovery Measurements**

Immediately on stopping:

- Find the pulse (it'll be much easier — the beat is strong after exercise).
- Count for 15 seconds × 4, or 60 seconds full count if they can sit still.
- Record: **HR immediately after exercise:** _____

At 2 minutes:

- Sit quietly, count again. Record: **HR at 2 min:** _____

At 5 minutes:

- Count again. Record: **HR at 5 min:** _____

At 10 minutes:

- Count again. Record: **HR at 10 min:** _____

---

**4. The Recovery Graph**

Plot the numbers on simple graph paper (or in a quick table):

| Time | Heart Rate (bpm) |
|---|---|
| Baseline (resting) | _____ |
| Immediately after | _____ |
| 2 min after | _____ |
| 5 min after | _____ |
| 10 min after | _____ |

Discussion:

> "Look at the shape of the recovery. Did it drop fast at first and then slow down? That's typical. The biggest drop usually happens in the first 2 minutes."

Calculate:

- **How long did it take to return to baseline?** That's your **recovery rate**.

> "Your loops are working. They sensed your muscles burning oxygen, ramped up the heart rate to deliver more, and then dialed it back down when the demand stopped. You just watched a feedback loop in action — but with numbers."

Save these baseline numbers carefully. You'll come back to them.

## Independent Practice

### Goal

Take resting heart rate measurements over the next several days to see how consistent your "baseline" actually is.

### Activities

**1. The Daily Resting Heart Rate**

For 5 days in a row, at roughly the same time each day (morning right after waking is ideal):

- Stay still for at least 2 minutes.
- Count your pulse for 60 seconds.
- Record it.

You should see your resting heart rate is roughly the same each day, with some variation. That variation is normal. Anything you do (sleep less, get excited, run around right before measuring) will affect it.

**2. The Variation Hunt**

Find at least ONE thing that changes your heart rate. Examples:

- Take your pulse right after sitting quietly.
- Take it again right after standing up.
- Take it again right after laughing or being startled (without making it artificial).
- Take it after a normal meal (about 30 minutes after).

Each time, record what changed and what your heart rate was.

### Bio-Telemetry Log

> **Day 1 Resting HR:** _____
> **Day 2 Resting HR:** _____
> **Day 3 Resting HR:** _____
> **Day 4 Resting HR:** _____
> **Day 5 Resting HR:** _____
>
> **The Most Interesting Thing That Changed My Heart Rate:** _____________
>
> **By how much it changed:** _____ bpm

**Sentence starters for younger learners:**
- "My heart beat faster when ___."
- "It went back to normal after ___."

### Reflection Questions

- Was your resting heart rate the same every day? What might explain the differences?
- What made your heart rate change the MOST?
- If you graphed your recovery after exercise, what shape did it make?

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Take their own pulse:** Demonstrate the wrist or neck technique without help.
2. **Explain what a baseline is:** "A starting measurement so I can tell if something changed." Or similar.
3. **Describe what recovery rate measures:** "How fast my heart rate goes back to normal after I exercise." Or similar.

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 4.

> "You just measured something about your body that you've never measured before. How does it feel to know that number?"

> "Most adults don't know their resting heart rate. You do. You also know how fast your hardware recovers from a small load."

This isn't a competition. The numbers are yours, and they're a starting point. Over the rest of the curriculum, you'll get more familiar with your own telemetry — what your body's "normal" actually looks like.

**This week's takeaway:** You can measure things about yourself. The body is not mysterious — it's a system with readable signals.

## Spiral Review

:::tip Connecting to Earlier Weeks
**From Week 1:** Your heart rate is a piece of firmware. You don't control it consciously; the brain runs the loop.

**From Week 2:** The heart-rate-during-exercise loop is a perfect example of negative feedback. Muscles need oxygen → heart rate climbs → demand met → heart rate climbs back down.

This week, you watched that exact loop and put numbers on it.
:::

---

:::tip Simplify (Ages 8–9)
Use the 15-second count × 4 trick. Only take three measurements: resting, immediately after exercise, and 5 minutes after. Skip the daily-tracking week. The single-day measurement is enough.
:::

:::tip Extend (Ages 10–12)
Have the learner take their resting heart rate first thing in the morning (before getting out of bed) every day for two weeks. They will see that on days when they slept poorly, the resting heart rate is often a little higher. This is the body's "I'm working harder than usual" signal. (Don't draw conclusions about sleep "quality" — just notice the correlation.)
:::
