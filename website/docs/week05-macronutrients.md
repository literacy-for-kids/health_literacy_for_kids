# Week 5: Structural Materials vs. Thermal Energy
*Unit 2 — The Chemical Plant*

Last week we tracked food from mouth to cell. This week we look at what the food is actually made of.

**Food contains three big fuel types: carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. They are not interchangeable. Each one is used by the body for different purposes. On top of that, food carries micronutrients — small amounts of vitamins and minerals — that act like helper molecules and cofactors. Without them, the chemical plant can't run, no matter how much fuel you put in.**

Diet is a chemical engineering problem, not a moral one.

---

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This week introduces the three macronutrients and the concept of micronutrients.
- The biggest pedagogical move: replace food-morality language with "what is this input doing in the system?"
- Protein is the most surprising one for kids — they usually think of food as energy, but protein is largely STRUCTURAL.
- Avoid all dietary prescriptions. The point is to understand the categories, not to grade their lunch.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Gather 4–5 packaged foods with nutrition labels. Anything works — a box of crackers, a yogurt container, peanut butter, cereal, a granola bar. Mix categories.
- Or use a simple list of common foods (bread, eggs, milk, peanut butter, broccoli, rice, salmon) if you don't have packages handy.
- Read through the protein-as-structural-material section ahead of time. It's the part learners find most surprising.
- **Important framing reminder:** No food is being judged this week. Stay neutral. Use the word "fuel" or "input" instead of "good" or "bad."
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
You may be tempted to insert "eat more vegetables" or "less sugar" while explaining macronutrients. **Don't.** Stay in the systems frame: this is what each fuel type does, this is what the body uses it for. Choices belong to the learner and their family.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "There are three big kinds of food fuel — quick-burn (carbs), slow-burn (fats), and building-block (proteins). You need all three for different reasons."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Skip the micronutrient deep-dive. Just mention that food also has tiny helper-bits called vitamins.
- Use 3 fuel types as concrete things, not abstract categories. Use specific foods as examples.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Use a campfire analogy: kindling = carbs (burns fast), big logs = fats (burns slow), bricks for a fire pit = protein (you can't burn bricks, they're for building).
- Drawings instead of writing.

**What success looks like:** The learner can name the three fuel types and give one food example for each.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Read actual nutrition labels. Calculate the ratio of macronutrients in each food.
- Discuss the fact that most foods have ALL three macronutrients in varying amounts — there is almost no such thing as a "pure carb" or "pure protein" food.
- Introduce the idea that proteins are made of amino acids, fats are made of fatty acids, and carbs are made of glucose-like sugar molecules.
:::

## Guided Session 1
### The Three Fuel Types
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- name the three macronutrients
- describe what each one is used for in the body
- give at least one food example of each

---

#### Activities

**1. The Campfire Analogy**

Draw a campfire on paper. Around it, draw three piles:

- A pile of **kindling** (small twigs)
- A pile of **big logs**
- A pile of **stone blocks**

Then explain:

> "If you want a fire RIGHT NOW, what do you light first?"

Kindling. It burns fast and bright but doesn't last.

> "What keeps the fire going for hours?"

Big logs. They burn slow and steady.

> "And the stones?"

The stones aren't burned at all. They're the fire pit. They're STRUCTURE.

Now apply this to food:

- **Carbohydrates** = kindling. Quick-burn fuel. Fast energy.
- **Fats** = big logs. Slow-burn fuel. Long-lasting energy reserve, also building material for cell membranes and certain hormones.
- **Proteins** = stones. Mostly used as STRUCTURE — muscles, enzymes, antibodies, hair, skin. Only burned as fuel when nothing else is available.

---

**2. Carbohydrates — The Quick-Burn Fuel**

> "Carbohydrates break down into glucose, which cells can use very fast. You can have glucose in your bloodstream within minutes of eating."

Examples of carbohydrate-heavy foods:

- Bread, rice, pasta, cereal
- Fruit
- Vegetables
- Candy, soda, juice
- Beans (also have lots of protein)

Carbohydrates come in two main types — simple (break down very fast) and complex (break down slowly). We'll go deep on this next week.

What the body uses carbs for:

- Immediate fuel for the brain and muscles
- Stored as **glycogen** in the liver and muscles for short-term reserves (a few hours' worth)
- Excess gets converted to fat for long-term storage

---

**3. Fats — The Slow-Burn Reserve**

> "Fats carry more than twice the energy per gram of carbs or proteins. They're how the body stores energy for the long term."

Examples of fat-heavy foods:

- Butter, oil, avocados
- Nuts, seeds, peanut butter
- Cheese, fatty fish, meat

What the body uses fats for:

- Long-term energy storage (you have weeks of energy stored as fat, even when you're not "fat-looking")
- Building cell membranes (every cell in your body has a fatty outer wall)
- Insulation (a layer of fat under the skin keeps you warm)
- Building certain hormones
- Carrying fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) — you can't absorb these without fat in the meal

Important to say:

> "Fat in the body isn't 'extra' or 'bad.' It's a critical part of how the system is designed. You'd literally die without enough body fat — your hormones wouldn't work, your brain (which is largely fatty tissue) wouldn't function, your cells would fall apart."

---

**4. Proteins — The Building Material**

> "Here's the surprising one. Proteins aren't mostly for energy. They're mostly for BUILDING."

Examples of protein-heavy foods:

- Eggs, meat, fish
- Beans, lentils, tofu
- Dairy (milk, yogurt, cheese)
- Nuts and seeds (also have lots of fat)

What the body uses protein for (this is a long list — make sure to read it slowly):

- Muscle tissue
- Skin, hair, fingernails
- Enzymes (the molecular tools that catalyze chemical reactions)
- Antibodies (the immune system's "virus definitions" — we'll cover these in Week 8)
- Hormones (chemical messengers like insulin)
- Hemoglobin (the molecule in red blood cells that carries oxygen)
- Receptors on cells (the docks that other molecules bind to)
- The structural framework of every cell

> "Protein is so important that if the body doesn't get enough, it will literally start breaking down its own muscles to get amino acids for more critical jobs."

Cells are constantly being built, repaired, and replaced. The body needs a steady supply of amino acids (the building blocks of protein) to keep doing this.

## Guided Session 2
### Micronutrients — The Helper Molecules
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- explain that vitamins and minerals are needed in small amounts to keep chemical reactions working
- give one example of a vitamin or mineral and what it's used for
- read a basic nutrition label and identify macronutrient amounts

---

#### Activities

**1. The Helper-Molecule Analogy**

> "Imagine a car engine. The fuel is gasoline — that's the macronutrients. But the engine also needs oil. Just a small amount, but if the oil runs out, the engine seizes. The car can have a full tank of gas and still not run."

> "Micronutrients — vitamins and minerals — are more like tiny helper molecules and cofactors. The oil analogy helps us picture their importance, but bodies are not literal engines. The body only needs small amounts. But if a key vitamin or mineral runs out, certain enzymes stop working, and the chemical plant grinds to a halt in specific places."

---

**2. A Few Key Examples**

These are illustrative — not a memorization list.

- **Iron:** part of hemoglobin, the molecule that carries oxygen in your blood. Without enough iron, the body can't make enough hemoglobin, and the result is tiredness because cells aren't getting enough oxygen.
- **Vitamin D:** needed to absorb calcium for bones. Your skin can actually make vitamin D when sunlight hits it — one of the few cases where the body manufactures a vitamin instead of needing it from food.
- **Vitamin C:** required to make collagen, the protein that holds connective tissue together. Sailors centuries ago who didn't get vitamin C developed scurvy — their connective tissue literally fell apart.
- **Calcium:** the main structural mineral in bones and teeth. Also used by muscles to contract and by nerves to fire.
- **B vitamins:** a whole family of vitamins that help cells convert fuel into ATP. Without them, the energy conversion slows down.

> "Different foods carry different micronutrients. The body needs the WHOLE menu — not just one thing in big amounts."

---

**3. Reading a Nutrition Label**

Pull out one of the food packages you gathered. Find the nutrition facts panel.

Walk through what's there:

- **Serving size:** how much they're describing.
- **Calories:** the amount of food energy listed for one serving.
- **Total fat:** how much of that energy is from fat.
- **Total carbohydrate:** how much is from carbs. Sub-categories: sugars (simple carbs) and fiber (carbs the body can't break down).
- **Protein:** how much is protein.
- **Vitamins and minerals (sometimes listed):** any micronutrients in significant amounts.

Pick 2–3 different foods and compare:

- Which is highest in carbs?
- Which is highest in fat?
- Which is highest in protein?
- Which has the most varied micronutrients?

Important to say:

> "We are NOT going to talk about whether the numbers are 'good' or 'bad.' We're just reading what's in the food. It's data, like the resting heart rate measurement from Week 3. Knowing the data is its own thing."

## Independent Practice

### Goal

Observe the macronutrient content of foods you actually eat and notice the variety in your normal week.

### Activities

**1. The Fuel Survey**

Look at the labels of three foods you ate this week. (Anything works.) For each one, record:

| Food | Carbs (g) | Fat (g) | Protein (g) |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |
| | | | |
| | | | |

**2. The Whole-Food Category**

Some foods don't have labels — fruit, vegetables, eggs, fresh meat, beans. Pick three of these foods and just GUESS which macronutrient category they fall into. Then look it up or ask the facilitator.

- Apple: mostly _____
- Eggs: mostly _____
- A handful of almonds: mostly _____
- A glass of milk: mostly _____
- A bowl of black beans: mostly _____

(For reference — apple: carbs. Eggs: protein and fat. Almonds: fat and protein. Milk: carbs, protein, and fat in roughly equal balance. Black beans: carbs and protein.)

### Bio-Telemetry Log

> **One food I ate this week that's mostly carbs:** _____________
>
> **One food I ate this week that's mostly fat:** _____________
>
> **One food I ate this week that's mostly protein:** _____________
>
> **One food I ate that had ALL THREE in balanced amounts:** _____________
>
> **One micronutrient I want to know more about:** _____________

**Sentence starters for younger learners:**
- "I noticed that ___ is mostly carbs because ___."
- "My favorite food has lots of ___."

### Reflection Questions

- Did any food surprise you with its macronutrient content?
- Which food did you eat the most of this week?
- If carbs, fats, proteins, and micronutrients do different jobs, what jobs do you notice in the meals you eat?

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Name the three macronutrients:** carbohydrates, fats, proteins.
2. **Explain what protein is mostly used for:** "Building stuff." (Muscles, enzymes, antibodies, etc.)
3. **Explain why micronutrients matter:** "They're like oil in an engine — small amounts, but you can't run without them." Or similar.

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 6.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
> "Once you know foods are mixtures of carbs, fats, proteins, and micronutrients, food-morality language becomes a lot less useful. Different inputs do different jobs."

This is a powerful frame shift. In this curriculum, foods are not moral rankings. They are combinations of energy, structure, and helper molecules. A piece of candy is mostly fast fuel. A piece of broccoli is mostly fiber and micronutrients. They do different things.

**This week's takeaway:** Diet is a chemical engineering problem. Different fuels do different jobs. Nobody is allowed to shame anybody — including themselves — about food choices in this curriculum. We just look at what each input does in the system.
:::

## Spiral Review

:::tip Connecting to Earlier Weeks
**From Week 4:** Last week you learned that food becomes ATP. This week you learned that food has three main fuel types, plus micronutrient lubricants. Each fuel type takes a different path to becoming ATP (or to becoming structural material instead of ATP).

**From Week 1:** Hunger is a system alert — but the body doesn't only need energy. It also needs structure and helper molecules. That's why eating only one kind of food for too long doesn't support the whole system very well.
:::

Have the learner pick one specific protein, fat, or carbohydrate (e.g., glucose, oleic acid, hemoglobin) and look up its chemical structure and one role in the body. Discuss the fact that these are all REAL molecules with specific shapes that determine what they can do.
