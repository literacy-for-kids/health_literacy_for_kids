# Week 16: Optimizing Your Physical Operating Environment
*Unit 5 — The Optimization Protocol (Capstone)*

Last week you wrote a protocol. This week you set up the room it runs in.

**A protocol is useless if the environment fights it. Engineers don't just write code — they also optimize the runtime environment the code runs in. If your protocol says "wind down at 9:30 PM" but your bedroom is lit like a kitchen and your phone is on the pillow, the environment is overriding the protocol. This week, you audit the physical and behavioral environment around your target variable, and then change one to three small things to give your protocol a fair chance.**

This week, like the rest of the capstone, is yours. You pick the changes. The facilitator asks questions; you decide what to modify.

---

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- The student has a target variable (from Week 15). This week, they map the environment around that variable and pick a few small things to change.
- CRITICAL: Do not prescribe environmental changes. You can ask "What's the light like in your bedroom at 9 PM?" — you cannot say "You should turn off the overhead light at 9 PM."
- Small and specific beats big and vague. One to three changes, max. A single phone-charging-in-another-room change is a real intervention.
- Costs and constraints are real. If a change requires buying something expensive or coordinating with other household members who aren't on board, it isn't going to happen. Help the student find what's actually feasible.
- Some factors will be outside the learner's control. Those still belong on the system map, and they are not reasons to blame the learner.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Re-read the student's Week 15 protocol. You need to know what variable they're studying before you can help them audit the environment around it.
- Resist the urge to suggest changes. The student lives in the environment; you don't. Their picks will be better than yours.
- If the student's target variable is sleep, food timing, focus, or anything involving the bedroom or a device, prepare for the conversation to involve screens. Stay neutral. Ask questions; don't lecture.
- Some constraints will be outside the learner's control. Keep those in the map anyway. No-control factors are still part of systems thinking.
- Changes should be low-cost and feasible. If a household constraint blocks a change, that is data, not failure.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
You are an environmental engineer's assistant. The student is the engineer. Your job is to help them see what's currently in their environment — not to tell them what's wrong with it.

**Best opening question:** "If we were going to map every single thing in your environment that affects your variable, where would we start?"
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "We're going to look at your room and your day and find one little thing we can change to help your experiment work better."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Skip the formal audit categories. Just walk through the space together and notice what's there.
- One change, not three. One is plenty for two weeks.
- The Bio-Telemetry Log entry can be a drawing of the "before" and "after" room/routine.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Take a literal walk through the relevant space (bedroom, kitchen, study area). Point at things. Ask, "Does this help you with your experiment, or does it get in the way?"
- The minimum viable change can be very small: moving a stuffed animal, closing a curtain, putting the homework folder somewhere new.

**What success looks like:** The learner picks one change to their environment and tries it for the next two weeks.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Use the full audit categories below (light, sound, temperature, devices, timing, food/drink, social).
- Encourage the student to write a short "if X, then Y" sentence for each environmental factor: "If the overhead light is on at 9:30 PM, then my eyes get bright-light signal and my circadian timing shifts later."
- Encourage one stretch change that involves coordinating with someone else in the household — practice in negotiating environmental changes is itself a skill.
:::

## Guided Session 1
### Environment Audit
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- map the environmental factors that currently affect their target variable
- distinguish factors that support the variable from factors that undermine it
- explain why "the environment" includes both physical conditions and behavioral patterns

---

#### Activities

**1. The Runtime Environment Concept**

> "Last week you wrote a protocol — a set of instructions for yourself. But a protocol doesn't run in a vacuum. It runs in an environment: the room, the lighting, the devices around you, the people in the house, the timing of meals, the noise level. We call this the 'runtime environment.' Software engineers spend almost as much time tuning the environment their code runs in as they spend writing the code itself. We're going to do the same thing."

Make it concrete with one example unrelated to the student's variable:

> "Imagine your protocol is 'read a book for 20 minutes every evening.' If the TV is on, your phone is buzzing, and your siblings are playing nearby — the protocol is going to lose. The runtime environment is overriding it. Same protocol, different environment, completely different results."

---

**2. The Audit Categories**

Pull out a piece of paper. Draw a vertical line down the middle. Title the left column "**Currently supports my variable**" and the right column "**Currently undermines my variable**."

Walk through these categories one at a time. For each, ask the student to think about their actual environment — not an idealized version. They can leave categories blank if nothing in that category is relevant.

| Category | Things to consider |
|---|---|
| **Light** | Light levels in the relevant space and time. Sunlight in the morning? Overhead lights in the evening? Lamps? Screens emitting light? |
| **Sound** | Ambient noise. Music. TV in the background. Conversations. Notifications. Sudden sounds (siblings, traffic, pets). |
| **Temperature** | Is the room warm? Cold? Stuffy? Does temperature change during the relevant time? |
| **Devices present** | Phone, tablet, laptop, TV, gaming console, smartwatch. Where are they physically located? Are they on or off? Notifications? |
| **Timing & schedule** | When does the relevant activity happen? Is the timing consistent or random? What comes right before it? What comes right after it? |
| **Food & drink** | What's eaten or drunk in the hour or two before the relevant activity? Caffeine? Sugar? Water? |
| **Social context** | Who else is present? Are they doing the same activity, or something different? Does their behavior affect yours? |
| **Pre-activity behavior** | What's the student doing in the 30 minutes before the relevant activity? Screens? Exercise? Conversation? Homework? |

> "We're not trying to fix anything yet. We're just looking at what's there."

Some factors will be under the learner's control, and some will not. Both belong on the system map.

---

**3. Specific Example — If the Variable is Sleep Consistency**

If the student is stuck, walk through this example. Don't impose it — just show what an audit looks like for one possible variable.

For a learner studying "how consistent is my bedtime and how rested I feel," the environment audit might look like:

**Currently supports:**
- Bedroom curtains block streetlight
- Pet sleeps in the room (calming presence)

**Currently undermines:**
- Overhead light on bright until I get into bed
- Phone on the bedside table with notifications on
- Last meal sometimes happens 30 minutes before bed
- TV plays in the living room until I go to sleep — I can hear it through the wall
- I scroll for ~20 minutes in bed before trying to sleep
- Bedroom is warmer than is ideal for sleep (~75°F)

> "Notice — we're not judging. We're just mapping. This is data."

---

**4. The Audit, for the Student's Real Variable**

Now do this for the student's actual target variable. Take your time. Walk through each category. Ask questions, but let the student write the answers.

Useful facilitator questions (notice they are questions, not prescriptions):

- "What's the light like in that room at that time?"
- "Where's your phone usually when you're doing this?"
- "What happens in the 30 minutes right before?"
- "Is the timing the same every day, or does it move around?"
- "Who else is around? What are they doing?"

When the audit is done, look at the two columns together.

> "What stands out? What's surprising? Anything you hadn't thought about before?"

## Guided Session 2
### The Minimum Viable Change
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- choose 1–3 environmental factors to modify
- describe each change as current state → target state
- identify constraints (cost, coordination, logistics) that could block the change

---

#### Activities

**1. Why "Minimum Viable"**

> "If we tried to change everything in your audit, we'd fail. Too many changes at once means: (1) we won't actually do them, and (2) if something does change in your variable, we won't know which change caused it. So we pick the smallest set of changes that could plausibly matter — usually one to three things."

This is a real engineering principle. Minimum viable change: the smallest modification you can make and still test whether it matters.

---

**2. Picking the Changes**

Look at the right column of the audit (factors that currently undermine the variable). Ask the student:

> "Of these, which ones do you actually have control over? Which ones would be easy or cheap to change? Which ones do you think would matter the most?"

The student picks 1 to 3 changes. The facilitator can ask questions — "What about that one? Why not that one?" — but doesn't pick for them.

---

**3. The Change Specification**

For each chosen change, write it out using this template:

```
ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE #___

WHAT I'M CHANGING:
_______________________________________

CURRENT STATE (what's happening now):
_______________________________________

TARGET STATE (what I want instead):
_______________________________________

WHAT'S REQUIRED TO MAKE THIS CHANGE:
(e.g., move a device, set an alarm, ask family member, buy something)
_______________________________________

CONSTRAINTS / OBSTACLES:
(what could get in the way?)
_______________________________________

WHEN IT STARTS:
_______________________________________
```

Examples of well-specified changes:

> **Change #1**
> WHAT I'M CHANGING: Where my phone charges at night.
> CURRENT STATE: Phone charges on my bedside table.
> TARGET STATE: Phone charges in the hallway outside my room.
> REQUIRED: Move the charger. Ask my parents if it's OK to charge it there.
> CONSTRAINTS: My alarm is on my phone, so I need a separate alarm clock OR ask my parents to wake me.
> STARTS: Tomorrow night.

> **Change #2**
> WHAT I'M CHANGING: Light level in my room after 9 PM.
> CURRENT STATE: Overhead light on bright until I get in bed.
> TARGET STATE: Switch to one small lamp at 9 PM, keep overhead light off.
> REQUIRED: A lamp (already have one).
> CONSTRAINTS: Sibling who shares the room sometimes wants the overhead on for homework. Need to negotiate.
> STARTS: Tonight.

---

**4. The Reality Check**

For each change, ask:

- "Is this actually realistic for the next two weeks? Not 'someday' — actually this week?"
- "Is anyone else affected? Do you need to talk to them first?"
- "What's the failure mode — if this change doesn't stick, what's most likely to break it?"

If a change has a major obstacle, either solve the obstacle now or pick a different change. A change with an unsolved obstacle is a change that won't happen.

---

**5. Add the Changes to the Protocol**

Open the Week 15 protocol in the Bio-Telemetry Log. Add a new section:

```
PROTOCOL v1.0 — ENVIRONMENTAL ADDITIONS

Change #1: _______________________________________
Change #2: _______________________________________
Change #3: _______________________________________

Start date for environmental changes: ___
```

The protocol now has two layers: the behavior you're tracking (or intervening on), and the environment you've set up to support it.

## Independent Practice

### Goal

Implement the 1 to 3 environmental changes. Document them in the Bio-Telemetry Log. Note what you expect to notice.

### Activities

**1. Make the Changes**

Today or tomorrow, actually do the things. Move the device. Buy or repurpose the lamp. Talk to the family member you need to coordinate with. Make the changes physical and real.

> "A change that exists only on paper is not a change."

**2. Write Down What You Expect**

Before you start collecting data on whether the changes helped, write down what you *predict* will happen. This is the engineer's version of "calling your shot."

Examples:

- "I predict that moving my phone out of my bedroom will help me fall asleep faster — maybe by 10–15 minutes."
- "I predict the lamp change won't actually matter much — but I want to test it."
- "I predict that eating dinner earlier will make my morning energy a little bit higher."

**3. Daily Notice**

For each of the next several days, in your Bio-Telemetry Log, jot down:

- Did the change actually happen yesterday/today? (yes / partial / no)
- If "no" or "partial," what got in the way?
- Anything you noticed about your target variable?

### Bio-Telemetry Log

> **Environmental change(s) I made:** _____________
>
> **What I expect to notice:** _____________
>
> **Day 1 — Did the changes happen?** _____________
> **Day 2 — Did the changes happen?** _____________
> **Day 3 — Did the changes happen?** _____________
>
> **Anything I'm noticing already:** _____________

**Sentence starters for younger learners:**
- "I changed ___ in my room."
- "I think it will help with ___."

### Reflection Questions

- Which change was easier to make than you expected? Which was harder?
- Were any of the changes blocked by something or someone? How did you handle it?
- Is there a change you decided NOT to make? Why not?

---

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Describe their audit:** "I looked at my environment for ___ and noticed ___ and ___ were getting in the way."
2. **Name their changes specifically:** "I'm changing ___ and ___ for the next two weeks."
3. **Explain the connection:** "I think these changes will help my variable because ___."

If the learner can do all 3 of these, they're ready for Week 17.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
> "Your environment is shaping your behavior right now, whether you've noticed it or not. The default arrangement of your room, your schedule, and your devices is already nudging you in certain directions. The question isn't whether the environment affects you — it's whether you're the one who designed it, or whether you inherited a setup somebody else picked."

> "You don't have to redesign your whole life. You changed one to three things. That's enough to test whether environment matters for your specific variable. It might. It might not. Either way, you're now an environmental engineer for your own body."

This week is quiet but powerful. Most people never audit their own environment. Most people don't even notice the environment exists. Now you do.

**This week's takeaway:** Your environment isn't neutral. It's nudging you toward something. Some nudges are changeable, some are not, and both are worth noticing.
:::

## Spiral Review

:::tip Connecting to Earlier Weeks
**From Week 11:** Circadian rhythms run on environmental signals — especially light. If your target variable is anywhere near sleep, the light cues in your environment are part of the system. You're now applying that knowledge directly.

**From Week 12:** Sleep architecture depends on the body running through its full maintenance cycle. The environment in the hour or two before sleep determines whether the body actually gets to start that cycle.

**From Week 3:** You took your baseline telemetry back in Week 3. The whole point of having a baseline is so you can compare what changes when you change something. The environmental modifications you make this week are exactly the kind of "something" your baseline is meant to be compared against.
:::

---

:::tip Simplify (Ages 8–9)
One change, not three. Walk through the relevant space together and pick the one most obvious thing to modify. The Bio-Telemetry Log entry can be a "before" drawing and an "after" drawing.
:::

:::tip Extend (Ages 10–12)
Treat the audit as a system diagram. Draw the target variable in the center of a page and draw arrows to it from every environmental factor — labeled with "+" for supports or "−" for undermines. Then, when you pick changes, you're literally removing or weakening arrows on one side of the diagram.
