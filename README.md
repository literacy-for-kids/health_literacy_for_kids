## Core Concepts

These mental models transition students from blindly following health rules to actively managing their physiological operating system.

- **Homeostasis is a Control Loop:** The body is constantly running sensor-actuator loops to maintain a stable internal environment. Sweating, shivering, and hunger are not just feelings; they are system alerts triggered when variables like temperature or blood sugar drift outside acceptable tolerances.
- **Fuel vs. Filler (Input Constraints):** We strip away the concept of "good" and "bad" foods, reframing diet as a chemical engineering problem. Calories provide raw thermal energy, but macro and micronutrients provide the structural materials and lubricants required to keep the engine from seizing.
- **Sleep is System Defragmentation:** Rest is not the absence of activity; it is a critical, biologically active maintenance cycle. Students learn that sleep is when the body clears metabolic waste from the brain, consolidates data (memory), and repairs physical micro-tears in the hardware.
- **The Immune System as Cybersecurity:** The body's defense network operates exactly like a firewall and antivirus software. It detects foreign hardware (pathogens), neutralizes threats, and writes new "virus definitions" (antibodies) to recognize future attacks.
- **The Microbiome as a Symbiotic Co-Processor:** The human body is not a single organism, but a host system. Trillions of microbes operate in the gut, acting as a third-party processing plant that breaks down complex fuels and synthesizes neurotransmitters. You have to feed the system that feeds you.

### The Curriculum Roadmap

This 14-week progression moves from baseline operational mechanics to long-term preventative maintenance.

### Weeks 1–3: The Control Loops

**Focus:** Baseline Telemetry and Homeostasis
We start by mapping the machine's baseline parameters. Students learn how the body uses negative feedback loops to regulate heart rate, oxygen saturation, and internal temperature. We look at what happens when environmental demands outpace the body's ability to self-correct.

- **Key Activity: The Bio-Telemetry Baseline** — Students measure their resting heart rate and respiration, then induce a controlled physical load (sprinting or jumping jacks). They time how long it takes for the system to recover to its baseline, tracking their own "hardware recovery rate."

### Weeks 4–7: The Chemical Plant

**Focus:** Fuel Processing, Energy Spikes, and Structural Nutrients
We follow the physical path of fuel. We explore how enzymes break down inputs, how glucose is transported through the circulatory system, and why simple carbohydrates cause rapid system spikes followed by crashes, while complex proteins provide slow-burn, structural energy.

- **Key Activity: The Fuel Audit** — Students track their physical energy levels and mental focus (the outputs) against the specific types of fuel they consumed (the inputs) over a few days. The goal is to identify the correlation between high-glycemic inputs and the inevitable afternoon system crash.

### Weeks 8–10: System Security & Threat Response

**Focus:** Virology, Immunology, and the Physical Hull
How does the body handle a breach? We look at the primary physical barriers (skin, mucus), the rapid-response "first responders" (white blood cells), and the adaptive memory cells that create long-term immunity. We also discuss how fevers are an intentional biological tactic to create a hostile thermal environment for invaders.

- **Key Activity: The Outbreak Post-Mortem** — Students analyze a common cold or minor illness they recently experienced, mapping the sequence of events from suspected hardware breach (exposure), through system alerts (sore throat/fatigue), active defense (fever/mucus production), to final resolution and system update.

### Weeks 11–14: Preventative Maintenance

**Focus:** Circadian Rhythms, Wear and Tear, and Sleep Architecture
We pull the concepts together by focusing on long-term hardware survival. We study the mechanics of the circadian rhythm, the role of light exposure in setting the body's internal clock, and the difference between running a system to failure versus executing daily preventative maintenance.

- **Key Activity: The Maintenance Protocol (Sleep Optimization)** — Students design a protocol to optimize their own "downtime." This involves engineering their sleep environment (manipulating ambient temperature, establishing a hard cutoff for blue-light inputs, and standardizing their boot-up sequence in the morning) and measuring the impact on their daily cognitive load.

### Weeks 15–18: The Optimization Protocol

**Focus:** Design, Test, and Iterate a Personal Health System
Students take everything they have learned and design a personal health maintenance system — a protocol covering sleep, fuel, and physical load. They implement it, collect data, and present their findings.
